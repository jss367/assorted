Assumptions behind the numbers

  • Equal starting cash: the larger of the buyer’s down payment plus purchase costs, or the renter’s deposit. Each option invests whatever is left. The deposit earns no interest and is fully returned at the end.
  • Equal monthly budgets: each month’s budget is the larger of the two housing bills. Both investment accounts earn your assumed return; the cheaper option adds that month’s savings at the end of the month. The model assumes you can afford either option and consistently invest the savings.
  • Mortgage: monthly interest uses the annual loan rate divided by 12. Payments stop when the loan is repaid. Mortgage insurance, if entered, is a fixed monthly amount until the scheduled balance reaches 78% of the original price; actual loan terms can differ.
  • Growth: home values and investments compound monthly at the equivalent annual rate. Rent increases once per year. Property tax starts as a percentage of the purchase price; tax, insurance, and homeowners association fees increase annually at the expense growth rate. Maintenance is a percentage of the home’s value at the start of each year.
  • Taxes: use an investment return after fees and taxes. Optional owner tax savings are your own fixed annual estimate, applied monthly. No deductions, tax brackets, or home-sale capital gains taxes are calculated automatically.
  • Costs and scope: purchase costs are paid upfront, and sale costs are a percentage of the future home value. Avoid counting escrow prepayments again in purchase costs. Shared utilities and moving costs are omitted, as are renovations, refinancing, rent concessions, and unexpected major repairs.
  • Beyond the spreadsheet: flexibility, stability, maintenance work, and the home itself matter too. These are hypothetical financial scenarios, not a prediction or an affordability assessment.
