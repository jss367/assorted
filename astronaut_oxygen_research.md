# Did Astronauts Use 100% Oxygen?

**Yes.** NASA astronauts breathed 100% pure oxygen in spacecraft throughout the Mercury, Gemini, Apollo, and Skylab programs.

## Why Pure Oxygen?

NASA chose a pure oxygen atmosphere at reduced pressure (~5 psi, about one-third of sea-level pressure) for several reasons:

- **Weight savings** - A single-gas system was significantly lighter than a dual-gas (oxygen/nitrogen) system, which was critical for reaching the Moon with the Saturn V rocket.
- **Simplicity** - Only a simple pressure sensor was needed to ensure adequate cabin pressurization, eliminating the complexity of mixing and monitoring two gases.
- **Decompression safety** - Pure oxygen at low pressure reduced the risk of decompression sickness (the bends) for astronauts.

North American Aviation (the Apollo spacecraft contractor) had suggested using an oxygen/nitrogen mixture, but NASA overruled this in favor of pure oxygen.

## The Apollo 1 Disaster (January 27, 1967)

During a launch pad test, the Apollo 1 cabin was pressurized with 100% oxygen at **16 psi** (higher than the 5 psi used in orbit, to maintain positive pressure relative to the outside atmosphere at sea level). A spark ignited cabin materials that had been soaking in this oxygen-rich environment, and the fire spread with extreme intensity. Astronauts Gus Grissom, Ed White, and Roger Chaffee were killed.

## Changes After the Fire

NASA made significant safety changes but did **not** abandon pure oxygen for in-flight use:

- **On the launch pad**: Apollo 7 (October 1968) and all subsequent missions used a mixed atmosphere of **60% oxygen / 40% nitrogen at 16 psi** during ground operations.
- **In orbit**: The environmental control system gradually replaced the mixed atmosphere with **100% oxygen at 5 psi** once in space, which remained standard for all Apollo and Skylab missions.
- **All ground tests** in 100% oxygen environments were reclassified as hazardous.

## Transition to Mixed Gas

- **Skylab** (1973-1974): Still used pure oxygen at 5 psi.
- **Space Shuttle** (1981-2011): Switched to a mixed-gas atmosphere (~21% oxygen, 79% nitrogen at near sea-level pressure), similar to Earth's atmosphere.
- **International Space Station**: Uses the same Earth-like mixed atmosphere.

The Russian Soyuz spacecraft has always used a mixed-gas environment, and NASA eventually adopted this approach starting with the Shuttle program.

## Summary

| Program | Atmosphere | Pressure |
|---------|-----------|----------|
| Mercury (1961-1963) | 100% O2 | ~5 psi |
| Gemini (1965-1966) | 100% O2 | ~5 psi |
| Apollo in-flight (1968-1972) | 100% O2 | ~5 psi |
| Apollo on pad (post-1967) | 60% O2 / 40% N2 | 16 psi |
| Skylab (1973-1974) | 100% O2 | ~5 psi |
| Space Shuttle (1981-2011) | ~21% O2 / 79% N2 | ~14.7 psi |
| ISS (1998-present) | ~21% O2 / 79% N2 | ~14.7 psi |
